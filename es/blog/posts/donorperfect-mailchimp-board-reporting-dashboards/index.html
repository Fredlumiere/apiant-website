Board-ready fundraising dashboards for community radio: DonorPerfect numbers, Mailchimp engagement, one view

Fred Lumiere Mar 5, 2025 6 minutos de lectura
#No lucrativo#DonantePerfecto#Mailchimp#MailConnect

The general manager of a community radio station should not be assembling the board deck on a Saturday afternoon. Yet every quarter, that is exactly what happens, because the financial numbers live in DonorPerfect and the engagement numbers live in Mailchimp, and joining them by hand takes a full weekend. The deck ends up being the same recycled layout with manually updated cells, presented at the meeting, and forgotten until the next cycle.

The board does not need fancy. The board needs accurate, current, and consistent. Quarterly reporting eats more staff time than it should because reconciliation between DonorPerfect and Mailchimp is manual, and manual reconciliation is fragile.

Why off-the-shelf tools fail

Most cheap connectors push contacts. They do not push aggregates. Total revenue, sustainer count, average gift, donor count by segment, these are calculated fields. If they do not exist as merge fields on the Mailchimp side, board dashboards stay in spreadsheets. The GM ends up either copying numbers from DonorPerfect into a slide every quarter or training a board member on DonorPerfect access (which most stations rightly avoid for security reasons).

The other problem is timing. A nightly sync is fine for monthly campaigns but useless during a 72-hour pledge drive. The GM needs to know how many new sustainers came in this morning, not yesterday’s snapshot.

A third issue is segmentation drift. Sustainer counts depend on consistent tagging. If a board-facing dashboard shows “sustainer count: 3,412” on Monday and “sustainer count: 3,408” on Tuesday because someone manually adjusted tags, board confidence in the dashboard collapses. Tags driven by Tags-from-Flags from DonorPerfect stay deterministic; tags maintained by hand do not.

The APIANT approach

MailConnect for DonorPerfect and Mailchimp does the calculation and the refresh frequency both:

  • Custom field selection carries up to 15 DonorPerfect fields per contact into Mailchimp. Donor totals (Lifetime Giving, Total Gifts, Largest Gift, Average Gift) sit on the contact record as merge fields and roll up automatically in Mailchimp’s reporting.
  • Actualización de datos cada 15 minutos keeps the numbers fresh enough that a Monday board meeting reflects Sunday night’s pledge drive results.
  • Dynamic donor segmentation keeps audience splits (new sustainer, existing sustainer, upgrade, downgrade, lapse) accurate without a re-segmentation pass.
  • Etiquetas de banderas de donantes carries sustainer status, upgrade status, and gift designation as Mailchimp tags so the dashboard can roll up by segment.
  • Respetar las preferencias de los donantes and cleaned email management maintain list quality, which keeps the engagement numbers honest.

Concrete steps

For the illustrative scenario of KCST Community Radio, a fictional community station with 8,700 Mailchimp subscribers and 3,400 sustaining members, the dashboard flow looks like this:

  1. Pick the right 15 fields. Lifetime Giving, Total Gifts, Last Gift Date, Last Gift Amount, Sustainer Status, Sustainer Start Date, Sustainer Monthly Amount, First Gift Date, Largest Gift, Giving Segment, Underwriter Status, Membership Level, Renewal Date, Days Since Last Gift, Pledge Drive Source.
  2. Map the tags. Tags-from-Flags for sustainer, upgrade-this-quarter, downgrade-this-quarter, new-this-drive, lapsed-this-drive. Five tags drive most of the dashboard splits.
  3. Sustainer health view. Mailchimp segment counts by tag: total sustainers, net change this month, average monthly amount, median tenure. One view, refreshed every 15 minutes.
  4. Pledge drive hourly view. During a 72-hour drive: count of gifts in last hour, count of new sustainers, average monthly amount, percent of drive goal, segmented by acquisition channel.
  5. Year-over-year comparison. Same quarter, same drive type, segmented by audience type. The merge fields are stable across years, so historical comparison is a saved view, not a manual rebuild.
  6. The 5 numbers the board actually asks about. Total revenue YTD, sustainer count net change, average gift, donor count net change, cost per acquired donor. Pin these to the top of the dashboard and let everything else live below.
  7. Read-only sharing. Board members get a shareable Mailchimp report link without needing seats. Quarterly meeting opens with the dashboard on the screen instead of a static slide.

Outcome

Representative numbers from comparable community stations after wiring this up:

  • Quarterly board deck prep time drops from roughly 12-15 hours to under 2.
  • Pledge drive decisions get made on hourly data instead of next-day data, which during a 72-hour event is the difference between adjusting on-air pitches mid-drive and learning the lesson next year.
  • Board engagement with the dashboard (clicks, time on view) becomes a leading indicator of board engagement with the organization overall.
  • Year-over-year comparisons stop being qualitative and become quantitative, which materially changes strategic conversations.
  • Underwriters who occasionally ask for fundraising health context get a clean, shareable view instead of an ad-hoc email thread that ties up the GM for hours.

The honest caveat: dashboards do not fix bad data hygiene. If DonorPerfect codes are inconsistent, the dashboard surfaces inconsistencies, not insight. Clean the codes first, then build the dashboard.

Donor privacy considerations apply even to internal board views. Aggregate counts and revenue totals are appropriate for board oversight; individual donor identities and amounts generally are not, unless the donor has explicitly opted in to recognition. A dashboard that surfaces “top 10 sustainers by lifetime giving” with names attached crosses a line most donors do not expect. Build the dashboard so individual identification requires a deliberate drill-down with appropriate access controls, not the default view.

What to keep out of the board view

Dashboards fail more often from too much than from too little. A board view with 20 charts loses focus; the same board view with 5 charts and an at-a-glance health indicator drives conversation. Vanity metrics belong out: total email opens, Facebook impressions, podcast downloads. They impress no one and they crowd out the metrics that actually inform decisions. The pledge drive on-air hours, the underwriter pipeline value, the cost per acquired sustainer: these are the numbers a board can act on.

A separate operational dashboard for the GM and the development director can carry the deeper diagnostic data. Keep the board view ruthless about scope. If a metric does not change a board-level decision, it does not belong on the board view. This discipline is easier to maintain when the data is right there in Mailchimp’s report builder and adding “just one more chart” is a click; the discipline has to come from the GM, not from the tool.

Builder takeaway

  • Pick your 15 fields with the board view in mind. Spending the slots on rarely-used data wastes them.
  • Un intervalo de 15 minutos para actualizar la información es el ritmo adecuado para las campañas de captación de fondos. Daily is too slow, real-time is overkill and noisy.
  • Five numbers, not fifty. Boards remember five. Anything more and the meeting loses focus.
