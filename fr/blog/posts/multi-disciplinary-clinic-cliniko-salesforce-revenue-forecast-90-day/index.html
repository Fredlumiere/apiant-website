A 90-Day Revenue Forecast for an Allied Health Clinic Built on Cliniko Invoices in Salesforce

Fred Lumiere Aug 18, 2025 4 min de lecture
#Santé#Cliniko#Salesforce#CRMConnect#For Enterprise

A finance manager rebuilding the forecast every Monday in a spreadsheet is a leading indicator that the business has outgrown its forecasting infrastructure. At a certain size, the spreadsheet is no longer the tool, it is the bottleneck.

Take an illustrative example: Southbank Integrated Health, a hypothetical single large clinic with 19 practitioners across physio, dietetics, psychology, and exercise physiology, and around $3.1M in annual revenue. The finance manager rebuilds the 90-day forecast every Monday by hand. Cliniko holds the invoices. Salesforce holds the pipeline. Nobody fully trusts the projection. NDIS-funded plans add a layer of recurring billable activity that the spreadsheet handles inconsistently. Leadership meetings spend a chunk of every Tuesday debating which number is right rather than acting on what the right number says.

Why the finance manager’s spreadsheet is the bottleneck

A spreadsheet forecast at this scale fails on three axes:

  • Freshness: it is accurate the moment it is built and stale within 24 hours.
  • Granularity: pivoting by practitioner or service line takes hours and the columns never agree on naming.
  • Completeness: funded-plan billable capacity (NDIS sessions remaining, insurer plan caps) is rarely modeled correctly, so the forecast either over-projects funded revenue that is already capped or under-projects renewals that are imminent.

The result is a forecast leadership debates rather than acts on. The cost is not the finance manager’s time (real as that is). The cost is decisions made slowly.

Why off-the-shelf tools fail

Bolting accounting software between Cliniko and Salesforce gives you tax-grade revenue recognition but not operational forecasting (the accounting layer is invoice-issued, not appointment-booked). A standalone BI dashboard on a Cliniko export gives leadership a chart but no operating surface inside Salesforce, where the sales pipeline and program activity already live. Splitting forecasting out of Salesforce creates two systems of record.

The APIANT approach

CRMConnect Cliniko to Salesforce streams Cliniko Invoices and Cliniko Appointments as native Salesforce custom objects, with Case Information (session limits, max invoiceable amount, case status) exposed alongside. The forecast becomes a Salesforce report built on three layers: confirmed invoices (recognized revenue), booked-but-not-yet-billed appointments (highly likely), and capacity-constrained funded plans (renewable revenue). The spreadsheet ritual disappears.

Concrete steps

  1. Enable Cliniko Invoices as Salesforce objects. Each invoice creates a Salesforce record with invoice number, issue date, amount, net amount, tax, discount, payment status, and patient. Status mirrors Cliniko (Draft, Pending, Paid, Voided).
  2. Enable Cliniko Appointments as Salesforce objects. Each appointment carries date, practitioner, location, type, status, and an expected billable amount derived from the appointment type. Status mirrors Cliniko.
  3. Enable Case Information sync. Capture program type, case status, session limit, sessions completed, max invoiceable amount, and remaining capacity for every active case. NDIS-funded plans in particular benefit from this, since the plan cap is the upper bound on forecastable revenue per patient.
  4. Build the 30/60/90 forecast in Salesforce. The forecast is the sum of three components: (a) Invoices in Paid status with issue date in the period (recognized), (b) Invoices in Pending status with issue date in the period (probable, weighted by historical pay rate), (c) Appointments in Booked or Arrived status with appointment date in the period, multiplied by the historical arrival-to-bill conversion rate.
  5. Add the funded-plan adjustment. For active cases on a funded plan, cap the forecasted appointment revenue at the remaining max invoiceable amount. This prevents the forecast from projecting billable activity beyond the plan ceiling.
  6. Build the practitioner and service line slice. Group the forecast by practitioner and by service line (initial assessments, follow-ups, group programs, package visits). This is the view that lets leadership see where the forecast is concentrated and where capacity is being underused.
  7. Configure the reporting cadence. Weekly forecast to the leadership team, automatically refreshed (no manual rebuild). Monthly variance review with finance, comparing forecast week 4 to actual.

Outcome

A clinic matching Southbank’s profile typically lifts forecast accuracy from the spreadsheet baseline of 75 to 82 percent (week-3 forecast versus actual) into the low 90s once the three-layer Salesforce forecast is running. The finance manager recovers 4 to 6 hours per week. Leadership stops debating the forecast and starts acting on it. NDIS-funded revenue gets projected correctly rather than under or overstated, which is the single biggest source of forecast variance in many Australian allied health practices.

Funded-plan tracking, done responsibly

Funded plan tracking benefits enormously from the Case Information sync, but it carries a governance requirement: the plan identity, funding source, and patient eligibility status are sensitive operational data, not marketing-safe data. Surface them in finance and operations dashboards. Do not surface them in marketing reports. The integration’s field-level configuration lets you make that distinction cleanly.

Builder takeaway

  • The forecast is a join of Invoices, Appointments, and Case capacity. All three need to be Salesforce objects before the dashboard is worth building.
  • Funded-plan ceilings are the most common source of over-forecast error. The Case max invoiceable amount field is what fixes it.
  • The right reporting cadence is weekly forecast, monthly variance. The integration makes both automatic.
