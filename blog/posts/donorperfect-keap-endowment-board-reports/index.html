Quarterly endowment reports in one afternoon: connecting DonorPerfect designations to Keap engagement data

Fred Lumiere Oct 14, 2024 6 min read
#Nonprofit#DonorPerfect#Keap#CRMConnect

Endowed scholarship reporting is the most labor-intensive task in scholarship fundraising, and almost none of the work is intellectual. A development associate spends two full days every quarter pulling DonorPerfect gift designations by fund, joining them by hand to engagement data exported from Keap, formatting per-scholarship sheets, and emailing PDFs to committee chairs. Then they do it again 13 weeks later.

The board does not need the work to be hard. They need the numbers to be right. The reason the work is hard is that DonorPerfect knows what people gave and to which fund, while Keap knows what they read, opened, and attended. Joining those two views by hand each quarter is the entire bottleneck.

Why off-the-shelf tools fail

Most integrations sync donor records and stop there. They do not preserve gift designation, restriction code, or fund identifier as structured Keap fields. The result: Keap knows John Smith opened the spring newsletter but has no idea John’s $50,000 endowment supports the engineering scholarship. The two halves of the donor story stay separated, which means the quarterly report stays manual.

Generic tools also tend to drop restriction language. Endowed scholarships have legal restriction text that has to be preserved on every report. Losing it is a compliance problem, not just a data problem. An audit finding that restriction language was misrepresented on a stewardship report is the kind of issue that ends careers, not just budgets.

The third gap is freshness. Endowment committees often meet near the end of a quarter, when the most recent two weeks of activity matters most (a major donor’s gift, a memorial pledge, a board chair’s stewardship visit). Nightly batch syncs are fine for monthly reporting and useless for end-of-quarter prep. Real-time-ish sync (15 minutes or better) is what lets the report reflect actual current state, not last week’s snapshot.

The APIANT approach

CRMConnect for DonorPerfect and Keap carries the fund metadata across as first-class Keap fields. The pieces that matter for endowment reporting:

  • Custom fields sync preserves DonorPerfect’s General Ledger Code, Restriction Code, Gift Purpose, and Fund Designation as Keap contact and gift fields.
  • CodeSync mirrors the fund dropdown list, so every endowed scholarship is a structured Keap value, not a free-text string.
  • Detailed donation information sync carries gift amount, designation, and giving history per donor per fund into Keap.
  • Real-time data sync keeps Keap fresh enough that the quarterly report does not require a final-night DonorPerfect export.

Concrete steps

For the illustrative scenario of Summit Scholars Fund, a fictional university-affiliated scholarship fund with 38 endowed scholarships and 5,200 alumni donors, the quarterly report flow looks like this:

  1. Mirror the fund taxonomy. In CodeSync, map DonorPerfect Fund Designation and Restriction Code to Keap custom dropdowns. The 38 endowed scholarships become 38 selectable values.
  2. Sync the fund-level fields. Enable sync for Gift Designation, Restriction Code, Gift Purpose, gift amount, and gift date on every gift record.
  3. Build per-scholarship Keap views. One saved Keap view per fund, filtered by Gift Designation = <fund>, showing donor name, lifetime giving to that fund, last gift date, last open date, and last event attendance.
  4. Layer engagement. Keap’s native engagement data (email opens, click activity, event attendance from the development calendar) sits next to the gift data because both are on the same contact record.
  5. Major donor watchlist per fund. Saved view: top 20 donors to fund X, sorted by days since last engagement. Any contact crossing 90 days quiet triggers a Keap task to the assigned development officer.
  6. Preserve restriction language. A read-only Keap text field per fund holds the gift agreement reference and restriction summary. It travels with every per-fund report so committee chairs see the legal context inline.
  7. One afternoon, not two days. The development associate opens each saved view, exports to PDF, attaches restriction text, and emails. Total prep time drops from 16 hours to 3-4.

Outcome

Representative numbers from comparable scholarship funds after deploying this pattern:

  • Quarterly report prep time drops from 16 hours per cycle to roughly 4, a 75 percent reduction.
  • Committee chair satisfaction (measured via post-meeting survey) improves because per-scholarship engagement context is included for the first time, not just dollars.
  • Major-donor disengagement is caught in weeks instead of quarters. The 90-day-quiet watchlist surfaces issues before they become lapsed-gift problems.
  • Compliance-review time on annual report drops because restriction language is structured, not buried in narrative.
  • Per-scholarship narratives written by development officers improve in quality because the engagement data sits next to the giving data inside one view, not in a folder of CSV exports.

The honest caveat: this does not eliminate the development associate’s quarterly review. A human still needs to read the data and write the narrative. The integration eliminates the spreadsheet plumbing, not the judgment. Foundations that expected the integration to replace the associate role have been disappointed; foundations that expected it to free the associate to spend more time on donor calls and less time on Excel have been satisfied.

The privacy and stewardship dimensions deserve attention. Scholarship recipients have a different privacy expectation than donors. Recipient stories used in reports should always be opt-in, with the right to withdraw. Many universities have specific FERPA-aligned policies on what can be shared with donors and committee members; the integration should respect those policies by structuring recipient data in fields that committee-facing reports do not pull from by default.

What to automate, what still needs a human

The temptation with this kind of integration is to push toward fully automated reports. Resist it. The numbers, the engagement summaries, the per-scholarship views, the major-donor watchlists: automate all of that. The narrative commentary that explains why one scholarship is over-performing and another is under-funded: leave that to the development associate. A board chair can tell the difference between data-with-context and data-without-context within the first 30 seconds of opening the report.

The other piece worth keeping human is the stewardship visit recommendation. The 90-day-quiet watchlist is a flag, not a decision. The right next step for a quiet major donor depends on context the integration cannot see: a recent personal loss, a known life transition, a previously-discussed timing preference. The development officer makes that call, not the dashboard.

Builder takeaway

  • Fund designation is the join key. Without it as a structured field, the engagement-to-gift join is impossible to automate.
  • Restriction language belongs as a field, not a footnote. Storing it on the fund record in Keap means it travels with every report automatically.
  • Catch quiet major donors with a 90-day rule, not a quarterly review. By the quarterly meeting, the relationship has already cooled.
